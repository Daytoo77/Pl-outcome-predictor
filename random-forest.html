A plain-language walkthrough

How a random forest decides

One decision tree is a self-built flowchart of yes/no questions. A random forest is hundreds of them, each deliberately handicapped so they disagree, voting together. Here is why that works — no code, no formulas.

1

What one decision tree does

Imagine the training data as a big pile of past matches. Each match carries a tag for what actually happened: home win, draw, or away win. The pile is a jumble of all three.

The tree’s whole job is to sort that jumble into smaller piles that are as pure as possible — a pile that’s nearly all home wins, another that’s nearly all away wins, and so on. It does this by asking one carefully chosen question at a time.

Choosing the first question

The tree looks at every feature it’s allowed to use, and for each one it tries every sensible cut-off. Take the feature home team’s rating minus away team’s rating. The tree asks: what if I split the pile at zero? At +40? At +90? For each possible cut it looks at the two piles that would result and measures how mixed each still is.

A cut that leaves two still-jumbled piles is useless. A cut that leaves “mostly home wins on one side, mostly not-home on the other” has genuinely separated the outcomes. The tree scores every feature at every threshold this way, and picks the single best one. That becomes the top question.

Why it keeps going

After the first split, each of the two piles is still somewhat mixed. So the tree repeats the entire procedure within each pile separately. The left pile might now split best on recent scoring form; the right pile on something else entirely. Then those piles split again. Every split makes the piles smaller and purer.

It stops splitting a pile when the pile is pure (all one outcome), or too small to be worth it, or a preset depth limit is reached. Each final pile — a leaf — is labelled with whichever outcome is most common in it.

Making a prediction: a new match walks down the flowchart, answering each question with its own values, until it lands in one leaf. That leaf’s label is the tree’s answer.

The catch with one tree

Grown deep, a single tree carves the data into tiny, hyper-specific pockets. It ends up memorising quirks — “matches in this narrow rating band, after a short rest, tend to be draws” — that were true of those 15 particular past matches and won’t hold up on new ones. One tree is knowledgeable but brittle.

2

What “random” means

A random forest builds hundreds of trees and deliberately handicaps each one in two separate ways, so the trees don’t all come out identical.

Randomness one — a different sample of rows per tree

Before growing a tree, you draw a random sample of the training matches with replacement: some matches get picked twice or three times, others not at all. Each tree ends up trained on roughly two-thirds of the real data, a slightly different two-thirds every time. No two trees have quite the same evidence, so a quirk that dominated one tree’s sample may be nearly absent from another’s.

Randomness two — a different handful of features per split

When a tree picks its next question, it is not allowed to look at every feature. At each split it’s shown a small random subset — say 6 of the 30-odd — and must choose the best question from only those 6. The next split gets a different random 6.

Why both matter

The second one is the subtler win. Without it, one very strong feature — the rating difference — would be picked as the top question by almost every tree, because it really is the best single split most of the time. Every tree would start the same way and look broadly alike.

And when trees look alike, their mistakes look alike — averaging near-identical things changes nothing. By hiding the strongest feature from most splits, you force some trees to build their logic around form, or shots, or league position instead. Now the trees genuinely disagree: each has found a different, partial view of what drives a result. The first kind of randomness stacks more variety on top. The goal of both is the same — make the trees’ errors unrelated to each other.

3

How the trees combine into one answer

For a new match, every tree in the forest independently walks it down its own flowchart and casts a vote — this tree says home, that one says draw, the next says home again. The forest tallies the votes, and the outcome with the most wins. (For a probability, it just reports the vote shares: 228 of 400 trees said home → 57% home.)

Why the average beats the best single tree

Every tree’s answer is part real signal and part random noise. The signal — “a big rating advantage really does mean the favourite usually wins” — is something almost every tree picks up, because it’s really there in the data. The noise is the tree’s own quirks: the odd threshold it landed on because of the exact matches in its random sample.

When you tally hundreds of votes, the signal adds up — most trees vote the same way for the real reason. The noise cancels out — one tree’s quirk says “draw here”, another’s says “away here”, and averaged across the forest these scattered errors mostly wash away. You’re left with mostly signal.

This only works because of Part 2. If the trees were near-copies of each other, they would all make the same mistake on the same match, and averaging identical mistakes still gives you that mistake. The randomness is what makes the errors independent, and independent errors are the ones that cancel.

4

An everyday version

The jar of jelly beans

A big glass jar of jelly beans is passed around a room of 400 people. Each person writes down a private guess at how many beans are inside.

Every individual guess is off — and not just a little. Some people are chronic under-guessers who never appreciate how many small things fit in a container; others always guess high. People near the window see the jar in good light; people at the back barely see it. Each person’s answer is shaped by their own blind spots, the same way each tree is shaped by its limited sample and its limited view of the features.

Yet when you average all 400 guesses, the result is famously close to the true count — usually closer than any single person, including the experts. The over-guessers and under-guessers pull in opposite directions and cancel. What survives the averaging is the part everyone got roughly right from actually looking at the jar.

A random forest is that room. Each tree is one biased guesser, looking at the problem through a narrow slot. The forest is the average of the room — and the slots are deliberate, because 400 people who all stood in the same spot and shared the same blind spot would just be one bad guess repeated 400 times.
