How it works

Reading the match before kickoff

Every number the model sees is knowable before the first whistle. Here is the path from 25 seasons of results to three probabilities, P(home), P(draw), P(away), drawn stage by stage.

  • Home
  • Draw
  • Away

9,074 matches · 78 pre-kickoff features · trained through 2022-23, tested on 2023-25, held out on 2025-26

01

One row per match, every column a "before" number

The dataset is one row per match. The target y is the result, H, D or A. Every feature is a difference: the home team's value minus the away team's, for things known the moment before kickoff: Elo gap, recent form, expected-goals form, days of rest, league position, squad market value, and the bookmaker's own price.

The one rule that governs the whole project: a feature for match k may only use matches 1 … k−1. Every rolling average is shifted back one game before it is computed, so a row never contains a trace of its own result.

02

Start from the market, not from zero

A bookmaker's price is the strongest single forecast in existence: it folds in every model, every team-sheet leak and every bet. So the model does not start from a blank slate; it starts from the market and only tries to improve on it.

Raw odds do not quite work as probabilities: 1/odds across the three results sums to a little over 1, the "overround", the bookmaker's margin. Divide it out and you have vig-free probabilities. Take their logs and you have the base margin, the number the trees begin from.

03

Model A: boosted trees learn what the market misses

A random forest builds hundreds of trees in parallel on bootstrap samples and takes a vote. Gradient boosting is the other idea: a chain of small trees, each one fitting the error the chain still has. Tree 1 nudges the market prior; tree 2 corrects what tree 1 left wrong; and so on for a few hundred rounds, each nudge tiny (learning rate 0.03).

Each tree is shallow, 3 questions deep, splitting on things like elo_diff ≤ 38 or xgd_ewm_diff ≤ −0.4. The leaves are not classes; they are small additions to the running log-odds. Add the market prior, add every tree, push through a softmax, and three probabilities come out.

04

Model B: a draw is what low scores look like

A draw is not a tactic a team picks; it is what happens when two low goal expectations meet: 0–0, 1–1, 2–2. Asking a flat classifier to carve that region out of overlapping distributions is hard. So Model B models the goals instead.

Two Poisson regressors predict λ = expected home goals and μ = expected away goals, each starting from the market's implied expected goals. From those, a whole scoreline matrix: P(i, j) = Poisson(i; λ) · Poisson(j; μ) · τ(i, j). The Dixon–Coles τ nudges the four lowest scores, where independent Poisson is known to be wrong. Sum the triangles and you have H, D and A: the draw falls out of the arithmetic.

05

Blend the two, then make the numbers honest

Model A and Model B make different mistakes, so a weighted average of the two beats either alone. The weight w is whatever minimises log loss on the tuning seasons. In the committed model it is 0.30 on the classifier, so the goal model carries 70% of the vote. The weight is not stable across tuning windows: the bundle tuned on 2021-23, which produced the test-season numbers, put about 0.925 on the classifier (reconstructed from the published test predictions).

Last step: calibration. A model can have a good log loss and still be slightly over-confident: it says 65%, it happens 58%. One temperature parameter T = 1.04, fit on the tuning block, gently rescales every probability so the stated chance matches the observed rate. Here the blend was already close, so T barely moves.

06

The Elo engine, upgraded

Elo is the strongest single pre-model signal: one number per club that moves after every match and quietly absorbs strength of schedule. Three changes over the textbook version:

Margin matters. A 5–0 and a stoppage-time 1–0 used to move ratings by the same amount. Now the update step scales with √|Δgoals|. Home advantage drifts. Instead of a fixed 60 rating points, each season is fed the home edge implied by the previous three seasons' results. Ratings regress. Every August each rating moves 25% of the way back to 1500, and promoted clubs enter at 1500.

07

Does it actually beat the market?

The bar is not "better than guessing home"; it is the bookmaker's own vig-free price. Log loss is the score that matters (lower is better; a flat one-third guess scores 1.099). Numbers below are on seasons the model never trained on.

The model does not "know" football. It knows where 25 years of results and 78 numbers disagree with the price, and moves a few points in that direction.

How to read a prediction. The companion tool, predictions, shows every held-out match: the blind probabilities, the most likely scoreline from the grid in stage 4, the feature signals that moved the pick, and whether it was right. The report works through the reasoning in full.
