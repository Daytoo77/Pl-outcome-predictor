Premier League Match-Outcome Model — a walk-through

From one match to the whole pipeline

You are predicting one of three things for every fixture — home win, draw, away win — from what was knowable at kick-off. This page starts with a single Arsenal match, shows exactly how its features are built without cheating, then zooms out to the six stages that turn 25 seasons of historical results into a classifier you can trust.

00

Start with one concrete match

Arsenal vs Stoke, 12 September 2015, at the Emirates. By the time this match kicks off, Arsenal have already played four league games this season. The model is not allowed to know the score (it will be 2–0). It is only allowed to know things that were already true at 15:00 that Saturday.

Here is Arsenal’s season so far — the four rows above the match we are predicting — and the one row we want a prediction for:

From those four prior matches the model computes, for example:

  • form_pts_roll4 = mean points over the last 4 = (0 + 3 + 1 + 3) / 4 = 1.75
  • gf_roll4 = mean goals scored = (0 + 2 + 0 + 1) / 4 = 0.75
  • ga_roll4 = mean goals conceded = (2 + 1 + 0 + 0) / 4 = 0.75

Stoke get the same treatment from their four prior games. The match becomes one row of home minus away differences, and the label H. Do that for every match across 25+ seasons and you have a training table. The rest of this page is how that table is built and checked.

02

How a rolling feature is built

“Last-5 form” sounds obvious, but the ordering of two operations is the whole game. You must shift by one match first, then average — so a row sees the five matches before it, never itself.

In the wild — Arsenal’s real form_pts_roll5 going into that Stoke match was 1.6 (from a run that still included the tail of 2014–15). After beating Stoke, Man United and Leicester 5–2, it climbed to 2.0 — but the model only ever sees the value before each match.
03

Why data leakage happens

Leakage is when information from the future — or from the match itself — reaches the model at training time. It produces a model that scores 70&%+ in your notebook and barely beats a coin flip on next weekend’s games. Two versions of the same experiment:

Subtle leak to watch — even with a date split, computing a rolling mean over the whole dataset first and splitting after lets test-season statistics bleed into training normalisation. Build features within a season, or fit any scaler on the training slice only.

Signal strength, ranked

A quick proxy for feature importance — how far each feature moves the home/away needle (|r with H| + |r with A|). The tree models will refine this, but the order rarely changes much.

Reading it — the top of this list is dominated by season-long measures (Elo, cumulative goal difference, league position). Last-5 rolling features matter but rank lower — recent form is noisier than a full-season picture. Head-to-head and rest days are weak on their own; they earn their place as tie-breakers.
05

Reading the confusion matrix

Once the model predicts on the held-out seasons, you cross-tabulate what actually happened against what the model said. Rows are the truth, columns are the prediction. The diagonal is correct; everything off it is a specific kind of mistake.

How to read each row

  • Row “actual H”: of 324 real home wins, the model got 247. It mostly confused them with away wins (59), rarely with draws (18). Home wins are the easy class.
  • Row “actual A”: similar story — 154 of 223 correct, main confusion is with home wins.
  • Row “actual D”: of 185 real draws, the model caught only 22. It called 96 of them home wins and 67 away wins. The model almost never predicts “draw”.

Why draws are the weak point

Three reasons stack up:

  • Base rate. Draws are ~25% of matches, the smallest class. A model minimising total error is rewarded for rounding them toward H or A.
  • No signature. §4 showed it — no pre-match feature correlates with a draw. A draw is often two well-matched teams plus a low-scoring game, and the second part is close to random.
  • Geometry. A draw sits between H and A in every feature that matters (Elo difference near zero, form difference near zero). Any small error pushes the prediction into the nearest decisive class.
What helps — class weights or resampling to stop the model ignoring draws; evaluating with log_loss / Brier score so a confident 40% P(draw) is rewarded even when the arg-max is still H; and treating a well-calibrated P(draw) as the real deliverable rather than the hard label. Getting draw recall from 12% to ~25% without wrecking the other classes is a realistic target — and a good iteration-loop goal.

Where this sits in the build. explore.ipynb produces the clean table, features.ipynb produces model_df.parquet with the features shown in §4, and model.ipynb does the chronological split from §3, trains the XGBoost model, and exports it.
